The main view

The news, as one object

Everything rests on this picture. A circle, lit as a sphere, divided into one region per running story — and the regions are not wedges from a centre, they're cells that grow and shrink and shove each other around as coverage moves. A slow week is a few fat continents. A frantic one is a mosaic.

Area is how much of the national press's attention a story is taking. Colour is how the press is framing it. And position is the part that turns a pretty graphic into an argument. Distance from the centre is how broadly the story is carried — the middle of the sphere is what the whole country is being told about, the rim is where one part of the press is out on its own. Direction is which part: a story drifts toward whichever corner of Fleet Street is driving it.

So a fat green continent in the middle is a big story everyone is covering warmly. A thin red shard on the right-hand rim is something only the right-wing papers are angry about. You can read both of those before you've read a single word.

Opening an outlet: the headlines themselves

Click again, on any outlet inside an opened story, and you reach the bottom: one shape per article that paper is running on that topic, each carrying its own score. This is the level that makes the whole thing checkable. Every number higher up is an average of these, and anyone who disputes a cell can descend to the actual headlines and see what produced it.

Area is prominence. Not "front page, section lead or standard", which is a print idea and unobservable from anything you can lawfully fetch, but the tier the headline occupied on that paper's own homepage — splash, lead, secondary, list item — read off the layout as described under Sources. Where an outlet can only be seen through its feed, prominence is unavailable and area falls back to a flat weight, marked as such rather than quietly averaged in. Colour is that article's own stance. Distance from the centre is still distance from neutral. What changes is direction, because every article here comes from the same paper, so the press map has nothing left to say. Direction becomes the desk: news at the top, comment to the right, leaders below, analysis to the left.

That turns out to be the most useful cut on the page. A paper's news reporting sits close to neutral while its position lives in the comment pages and the leader column — in the example above, the Metro's news pieces score around −1.0 and its leader −1.7, from the same newsroom on the same day. An outlet-level average that silently blends those two is measuring something nobody actually reads. Splitting them is not a nicety; it is the difference between "this paper is hostile" and "this paper reports it straight and editorialises hard", which are very different claims.

The headlines at this level are generic placeholders generated from a handful of templates, not real or imitation copy from any newspaper. In production they are the actual headline and standfirst, linked out to the source.

Why not a pie

A pie fixes every slice to the same centre, so it can only ever say one thing — share. Freeing the cells from that centre buys you a second and a third dimension for nothing: the layout can carry who, and the shading can carry how. The technical name for the layout is a weighted Voronoi treemap; the reason to use it here is that the result is legible in about two seconds and stays legible when the data changes underneath it.

The sphere lighting is deliberately cosmetic and deliberately harmless: the cells are laid out on a flat disc so their areas stay honest, and the roundness comes purely from a light source. Nothing is foreshortened, so nothing is distorted — you get the presence of an object without paying for it in accuracy.

Both claims are checked rather than asserted. Across the forty shapes above, every cell's painted area lands within 1.4% of its true share, none collapses to nothing, and the rank correlation between how broadly a story is carried and how close to the centre it actually renders is +0.91. Those are the numbers to regression-test when the data starts moving — a graphic this confident is only worth having if the geometry is telling the truth.

What the grading inside a shape means

A flat fill would colour each story by its mean stance, and that hides the most interesting thing on the page. The net zero rollback is the clearest case in the set below: the Guardian sits at −0.6 and the Mail at +1.1, but the average is near zero — so a flat cell would render it pale and calm, visually identical to a story nobody has strong feelings about. The single most contested issue would look like the dullest.

So each shape is graded along the same left-right axis the globe already uses for position. Fit stance against an outlet's place on the press map, weighted by how hard each is running the story, and paint the fitted value at each end of the shape. The slope is the polarisation. Consensus stories come out flat; contested ones sweep from green to red inside a single cell, and you can see which end belongs to which half of the press because it matches the direction the whole globe is already organised by.

Five of the forty stories here cross the sign line: the net zero rollback, Channel 4's future and airport expansion, where the left is hostile and the right favourable; the licence fee and the assisted dying bill, the other way round. Worth noting that crossing zero and being most divided are not the same — small boats has a wide spread and never crosses, because the whole press is hostile and only the degree varies. The gradient shows both cases honestly, which a mean cannot.

Opening a story

Click any cell and it lifts off the globe, which sinks and blurs behind it. The cell keeps its own outline as it rises, rounds out, and becomes a sphere in its own right — then resolves into a second map. One cell per outlet now, and the grammar is deliberately identical: area is how hard that paper is running this particular story, colour is that paper's own framing of it, and direction is still its place on the press map.

Only what distance means changes. On the globe it is how broadly a story is carried; here it is how far a paper's framing sits from neutral. The calmest coverage pulls inward, the sharpest sits out toward the rim — so the shape of the second sphere tells you whether a story has a cool centre with the heat at its edges, or whether nobody is playing it straight.

That reading is softer than the globe's, and it is worth saying so. A story typically has five to thirteen outlets on it, and a space-filling disc cut into that few cells has little radial freedom — every cell reaches the rim. Ranking rather than scaling the radius keeps the ordering from inverting (mean rank correlation +0.60 across the story set, never negative, against +0.92 on the globe), but at this level colour and direction carry most of the meaning and the table beside the sphere carries the exact figures. Forcing it harder was tried and thrown away: it produced a picture that was geometrically obedient and no longer honest.

Reality check

Three cells, populated for real

Everything else here is schematic. Three cells are not. Open Small boats & asylum and then Sky, Telegraph or Express, and you are looking at real headlines published in the week to 8 September 2026, scored by the method this plan describes against an explicit stance target: favourability toward people crossing in small boats and toward asylum seekers.

The Telegraph4−0.9−0.3 to −1.7
Express10−1.5−1.0 to −1.9

That is a spread of 2.3 points on a 4-point scale, on one issue, in one week — which is exactly the signal the product exists to show. But four things about how it was obtained matter more than the numbers.

Most publishers block AI crawlers

The Metro was asked for first and could not be fetched at all. Nor could the Telegraph, the Express, the Guardian or the BBC. Only Sky served its RSS directly. The Telegraph and Express figures above had to come through Google News's public feed instead. This is a live constraint the source table does not capture, it varies by publisher and by user agent, and it is getting more common. Check each outlet's robots.txt for the agent you will actually use before you promise an outlet list, and treat a blocked outlet as excluded rather than empty.

The samples are not comparable, and that is the point

Sky's two items are everything in its UK and Home feeds that day. The Telegraph and Express sets are topic-searched across about a week. A search for "asylum" returns campaigning coverage at a far higher rate than a general news feed does, so some of that 2.3-point gap is sampling, not editorial line. A real pipeline must take whole feeds from every outlet over the same window and let the issue classifier do the filtering — never search per topic per outlet, or the measurement is contaminated before scoring starts. The direction here is unsurprising; the magnitude should not be quoted.

Headline-only is thinner than planned

Google News supplies no standfirst, so the Telegraph and Express items were scored on the headline alone — a weaker input than the headline-plus-standfirst the plan specifies, and one reason several of those scores carry low confidence.

The mock had one assumption backwards

The synthetic data assumes comment and leaders carry a paper's position while news reports it straight. In the real Express sample the opposite holds: its news pieces average −1.6 and its comment pieces −1.3. "Fury as Home Office plans to outnumber villagers 10 to one" and "The UK town where locals 'don't feel safe'" are filed as news. The position is in the news pages, and an instrument built on the assumption that it lives in the comment pages would systematically understate exactly the outlets it most needs to measure.

Which is the strongest argument yet for the desk split at level three. Do not average an outlet into one number until you have looked at whether its news and its comment agree.

A last caveat: those scores are one scorer's judgement, uncalibrated and unreviewed. That is precisely what the gold set is for.

Playback

The archive is the product

A snapshot of today's front pages is a curiosity. The same picture running from March to September is an argument: you watch shapes swell and collapse, watch the colour of a shape turn as the press changes its mind, and read off the timeline underneath what happened on the day it turned. The strip beneath the sphere above does exactly this on synthetic data — press Play, or drag it, or step between event pins.

Playback is not a feature bolted onto the front end. It sets three requirements on the daily build that cannot be retro-fitted cheaply, because they govern what gets written into the archive in the first place.

1. Cells must keep their identity between frames

Fitting the sphere from scratch takes 160–350 ms for the layout and another 120 ms to draw — roughly three frames a second, which is not playback. It is also the wrong answer visually: an independent fit for each day puts a story somewhere different every frame, and the sphere boils rather than flows.

Both problems have the same fix. Each frame starts from the previous frame's seed positions and weights and runs a short correction rather than a full solve: 4–14 ms instead of 160–350, and the cell stays where it was because that is where the optimiser began. The consequence for the backend is that the seed geometry is part of the archive, written alongside each frame, not recomputed on read.

Measured on this page: 72 ms median per frame at the globe level, 49 ms inside a story — 14 and 20 frames a second, with the disc drawn at reduced resolution while it runs and at full size the instant it stops.

2. Position must be driven by something that moves slowly

The static sphere takes a cell's distance from the centre from a rank. Under playback that is a trap: two stories swapping rank teleports both cells across the disc. Over 120 frames of the same synthetic history, a rank-driven radius produced a worst-case seed jump of 0.62 of the disc radius against 0.35 for a continuous key. Radius therefore comes from breadth of coverage — how many outlets are running the story at all — which changes by one outlet at a time.

3. Stories need a floor, and a birth and a death

A cell allowed to shrink towards nothing takes the whole fit down with it. Once its painted area reaches zero the weight gradient, which is bounded by the target share, is far too weak to bring it back: in testing, a run that let stories decay freely finished with over 100 collapsed cells out of 40 × 120. Two rules prevent it — every live story keeps a minimum share, and a cell that is squeezed out entirely gets a hard weight kick until it reappears.

That makes the entry and exit policy an explicit design decision rather than an accident of the numbers, and it is implemented rather than merely specified. No issue is exempt. If the NHS is not being written about this week it is not on the globe this week, the same as anything else. What differs between issues is the duty cycle, not the privilege, and it is derived from the issue's own volume rather than assigned: a big issue runs in long spells with short gaps, a small one appears now and then. Every arrival and departure ramps over four days, so a cell grows out of the gutter and shrinks back into it rather than appearing between two frames.

Labels have to be damped, not just placed

The placer is a search: it scores every candidate position for a label on how much clear space it has. Re-run from scratch sixteen times a second, that search is unstable — a hair's change in a cell flips which position scores best and the label teleports across the shape. Left alone it produced a mean label movement of 8.9 px a frame, a 95th percentile of 33 px, and a jump of more than twelve pixels on 31% of all label-frames. That is the twitch.

Three dampers, all of them in force only while the globe is moving, so a still globe still gets the exact placement: a bonus in the scoring for staying near where the label was, which fades out over about thirty pixels so a genuine move still wins; easing the label thirty per cent of the way to its new position each frame instead of snapping; and a size hysteresis, so type only resizes when the change is worth more than a pixel. A label already up also keeps its slot until its cell falls well out of contention, which stops labels blinking on and off at the boundary. Together: mean movement 1.4 px, 95th percentile 3.8 px, and jumps over twelve pixels on 0.5% of label-frames.

A fourth damper turned out to be needed for colour rather than position. Each label picks black or white ink by reading the shading underneath it, and that reading was one pixel. Under playback the pixel drifts across a lit gradient, so any label sitting near the threshold flickered between black and white — measured at 2.8% of label-frames, concentrated on a handful of labels that happened to sit on the terminator. The ink is now read from an average of the patch the label actually covers, and once chosen it holds until the reading crosses well past the threshold rather than merely over it. That is 0.08% of label-frames, and no label changes ink more than twice in a run — which is a real cell moving from the lit side to the dark one, not chatter.

One trap in that: clearance is tested where the placer puts a label, not where the easing leaves it a frame later, so easing could reintroduce the overlap the placer exists to prevent. Anything that would touch on the way is dropped straight onto its target, which keeps "no overlap" a guarantee rather than a probability. Over a hundred sampled frames of playback the repair never had to fire and no two labels overlapped.

Getting that right took one correction worth recording. A first pass exempted the sixteen largest issues outright, which is the kind of shortcut that looks harmless and is not: the picture it produces is one where the big issues are structurally incapable of leaving. The second pass removed the exemption but kept a four-day ramp at each end of a spell, and the biggest issues still never left — their off-gaps were shorter than the two ramps, so their share never actually reached the floor. Uniform-looking rules can hide an exemption inside their constants. A gap now has to outlast both ramps, and on the history above every one of the forty issues has days when it is off the globe: seventeen for small boats, sixteen for the NHS, fifty-six for the licence fee. The live count moves between eighteen and forty.

The mechanism that makes this survivable is that cells are matched across frames by issue, not by position in an array. A surviving issue inherits its own seed and weight; an arriving one starts at its anchor with the smallest weight in play. Get that wrong and every entry or exit reshuffles the whole sphere.

What the timeline is showing

A comparison, chosen by the reader rather than by us. Turn on Select Issues and clicking a shape adds it to the chart instead of opening it; click its chip to drop it again, or turn the toggle off to go back to drilling down. Each selected issue gets a line: x is time, y is its share of everything the nationals published that day, and the line takes its own colour purely so you can tell it from the others. Stance deliberately does not travel onto this chart: with several lines crossing, identity matters more than shade, and carrying the red-to-green scale here would leave you able to read neither. The sphere still holds the stance reading, and the playhead ties the two together.

Every line sits on one shared scale. Rebasing each to its own average would make the small issues legible, but it would also make a two per cent story look like a twenty per cent one, and the whole point of the sphere is that share of attention is the quantity that matters. A line stops where its issue left the globe and starts again where it came back, so the gaps are information too. The pins on the rail above are events; a pin belonging to a selected issue is filled rather than hollow.

Every line is also scanned for local peaks — a point that is the highest within six days and stands a fifth clear of the troughs either side, which keeps noise out — and as the playhead reaches one, a caption appears over it in that line's colour. Where an event sits under the peak it supplies the wording; where none does, the peak describes itself. It is the same detection the backend would run to generate the event feed, done here on the client because the series is already in memory.

Where the event flags come from

Not from an editor. Hand-curating a daily events feed is the kind of task that quietly becomes a job, and it would be the only manual step in the whole pipeline. The flags are detected from the archive instead, on three signals:

  • Volume break. A story whose share jumps more than three standard deviations above its own trailing fortnight.
  • Entry. A story appearing above the floor that was not in the live set the day before.
  • Stance break. A shift in an issue's mean stance, or a sudden widening of the left-to-right spread, of more than 0.4 in a day. This is the most interesting of the three, because it catches the moment the press turns rather than the moment it gets loud.

Detection is arithmetic over data already on disk, so it is free. Only the label needs a model: one Haiku call per detected event, given the cluster of headlines from that hour, returning a single sentence. At a handful of events a day that is well under a dollar a month, inside the budget already set out below.

The honest-history problem

The taxonomy will change. Issues get added, split and merged, and — as the stance target note below records — occasionally corrected outright. That leaves two possible histories: as it was scored at the time, and as today's taxonomy would score it. They are different pictures and a scrubber that silently mixes them is lying.

The resolution is to keep the raw text and the scored output separately, stamp every frame with the taxonomy version that produced it, and treat a taxonomy change as a bounded backfill job rather than an edit. A re-score of a full year at roughly 2,000 items a day costs $95–190 — affordable once or twice, not casually. Playback should mark a version boundary on the timeline rather than smooth over it.

What it costs to serve

Very little. A frame is about forty stories × six numbers — share, seed x and y, weight, mean stance, stance spread. Seventeen frames a day for a year is under 10 MB raw, and quantised and gzipped into monthly shards it is a few hundred kilobytes each, served as static files from Pages with the adjacent month prefetched. The fit runs in the browser; nothing is rendered server-side.

One paper's whole front page

Inside a paper's headlines there is an All Issues toggle, and it turns the view into the same object as the main globe restricted to one masthead: every issue that paper is running, sized by how much of its own output goes on each, coloured by its own line rather than by a left-to-right spread. Angle and radius are borrowed from the national globe, so a shape sits where it sits nationally. That is the whole trick: read the two side by side and the difference between them is that paper's editorial priorities rather than the country's. The Express globe is mostly small boats, Reform and the Royals; the Guardian's is not.

The chart follows, as it does everywhere else: at this level each line is the share of that paper's output going on an issue. Clicking a shape opens that paper's headlines on it, and the toggle flips straight back.

Where it does not apply

Playback drives the globe, the story level and a paper's own globe, all of which are quantities that flow. The headline level is a list of discrete articles with their own births and deaths, and interpolating between two sets of headlines is meaningless. At that level the timeline keeps running but becomes a different instrument: a strip showing when each headline appeared and how long that outlet kept it up.

Sources

Getting the text, legally

Nearly every UK national still publishes RSS, and that is the backbone. The complication is that the three outlets most worth reading on politics — the Times, the Telegraph, the FT — put the body behind a paywall, so you get a headline and a standfirst and nothing else.

That looks like the project's central constraint, and it is narrower than it appears. Two of the globe's three encodings — area and position — need counts and bylines, not prose, so the paywalled titles are already first-class citizens on both. Only colour reads words.

Decided: headline and standfirst, for every outlet, always. Comparability by levelling down rather than by exclusion, so nobody is scored on a different instrument. It is a smaller sacrifice than it sounds — the headline is the most editorialised sentence in the paper, written in-house to frame the story for that readership, while the body is frequently wire copy running verbatim in five other titles. If you want to know what the Express thinks, the headline is where it lives. The RSS standfirst adds another 15–30 words of the most position-bearing text there is.

The thing to measure before trusting it: headlines are written hotter than articles, so headline-only scores may sit systematically low. Score a few thousand articles both ways where you have full text, and check whether the offset is a stable constant. If it is, subtract it. Random error does not matter much here — a cell mean over 30–100 articles averages it away at one over root n — but a bias does not average away at all. That is the whole test.

The legal line

Consuming RSS and linking out is ordinary, accepted behaviour. Storing full text in your own database to analyse it is a different question. The text and data mining exception in UK copyright law (s.29A CDPA) covers non-commercial research only — so if this ever becomes a business, that exception stops covering you and you should take proper advice before it does.

Practical rules that keep you well inside the line: never republish an article body, cap displayed snippets at a headline plus a sentence, always link to the source, honour robots.txt and a polite crawl rate, and identify your bot with a contact address.

The front pages you are not allowed to fetch

A feed cannot tell you how prominently a story was run — that lives on the homepage, and the homepage is exactly the request publishers defend. Which appears to close the prominence question for half the list. It does not, because those homepages have been photographed for years by the Internet Archive, and an archived page can be read whether or not the publisher would serve it to you today.

Three caveats, in order of how much they matter.

  • The Guardian returned nothing — bare domain, www, /uk, full https form, and a 2024 date. That may be an exclusion or an artefact of the availability endpoint; settling it needs the archive's CDX index. Check it first: a missing Guardian knocks a hole in the left half of the press map, and every claim about left-versus-right prominence depends on it.
  • Sampling rate is the archive's, not yours. Sky is captured sub-hourly, the Express about daily. So archived snapshots support comparing how prominent a story was at a given hour across outlets, and do not support comparing how long each outlet kept it up. Persistence stays a forward-collection-only measurement.
  • URL form is a trap. telegraph.co.uk returns a snapshot while https://www.telegraph.co.uk/ returns nothing; dailymail.co.uk is empty but /home/index.html works. These have to be pinned per outlet by experiment.

Joining the archive to the feed

The two sources are complementary rather than competing, and joining them is what makes the sparse one useful. An archived homepage supplies position and nothing else — it is a picture. The feed supplies identity, text, and a publication time. Match a headline in a snapshot to a feed item by URL and you have not added an article, you have added a prom_tier to an article you already hold and have already scored. Nothing needs re-scoring, and the archive never has to be treated as a text source, which keeps it well clear of the copyright question.

That join also answers the sampling problem above, which otherwise looks fatal for duration. The trick is not to measure dwell from the archive — there are nowhere near enough snapshots — but to use the archive as ground truth for calibrating a cheap continuous proxy. Your own hourly poll of an outlet's top-stories feed gives a dense signal: present or absent, and at what position. The archive gives an occasional, exact reading of what that hour's homepage actually looked like. Where the two coincide, fit the mapping from feed position and item age to homepage tier; then apply it to every hour where only the feed exists. Sparse truth calibrating dense proxy is a well-behaved arrangement, and it degrades honestly: the confidence in an outlet's dwell figure is a function of how many joined observations you have for it.

Three things decide whether it works for a given outlet, and all three are testable in week one:

  • Is the feed curated or merely chronological? If a top-stories feed is strictly reverse-chronological, position carries no editorial signal and the proxy collapses to present-or-absent. For a high-volume outlet that is close to useless: items fall out of a thirty-item feed in an hour because of publication rate, not importance.
  • Are there enough joined observations? Sky, captured sub-hourly, offers hundreds a month. The Express at roughly one a day offers about thirty — thin, but enough for a coarse three-tier mapping if not a fine one.
  • Are the snapshots spread across the day? This is the subtle one. If an outlet is only ever captured at two in the morning, the mapping is learned from night-shift layouts and then applied to the whole day. Check the hour-of-day distribution of an outlet's snapshots before trusting anything fitted from them.

One thing in this chain is asserted rather than measured: that headline type sizes really do fall into clean discrete ranks on a real page. prominence_probe.py, alongside this document, renders a homepage and gap-splits its headline sizes so the assumption can be checked in an afternoon. A clean result is three to six tiers with a small top tier and a long tail; a continuum means that outlet needs container position instead of type size.

Pipeline

Two loops, different clocks

Everything runs on GitHub Actions, hourly between 06:00 and 22:00 — seventeen runs a day, which sits inside the free tier even on a private repo. Ingest and analysis are separate jobs on the same beat, so a feed outage never blocks the site and a bad model run never loses you articles.

Issues

Clustering finds stories. A taxonomy makes them comparable.

Pure clustering is the obvious approach and it fails in a specific, frustrating way: the clusters are excellent but their labels are unstable. Tuesday's "NHS winter pressures" cluster becomes Wednesday's "A&E waiting times" cluster, and you cannot draw a line through them. You end up with a great daily snapshot and no time series.

So do both, in two passes.

Pass one — stories

Embed each article (headline, standfirst, first few paragraphs) and cluster within a rolling 72-hour window. HDBSCAN over the embeddings, or simple incremental centroid matching at a cosine threshold — the naive version works fine at this volume. A cluster is a story: one event, covered by many outlets.

Which of the two the globe draws

The shapes are stories. "UK sanctions on Israeli settlements", not "Middle East". Most run a few days; a handful run for weeks. The globe should look different on Tuesday from how it looked on Monday. One showing the same forty labels every morning would be a dead object.

The standing list below is a label attached to each story, and is never drawn. It is what makes March comparable with September: a story cannot carry a six-month line because it does not live that long, and its subject can. The chart under the globe can show either — a story's two-week spike, or a subject's slow drift, which is the same coverage seen from further away.

Pass two — issues

Map each story onto a fixed, hand-curated taxonomy of about 30 standing UK issues: NHS and social care, immigration and asylum, cost of living, housing, crime and policing, education, Ukraine, the Middle East, climate and net zero, the economy and taxation, devolution, and so on. Do the mapping with a cheap model, allowing multi-label and an explicit "none of these".

The taxonomy is deliberately stable and boring. That stability is the entire point — it's what makes March comparable to September, and it's what lets a reader trust that the line moved because coverage moved, not because your clustering wobbled.

Every issue must name its stance target. "Net zero rollback" is an event, not a target — favourable to what? The rollback, or net zero? Get this wrong and the sign inverts: the Guardian is hostile to a rollback and the Telegraph welcomes it, so a label that leaves the referent implicit will have half your outlets on the wrong side of zero. The convention here is that stance is favourability toward the thing the label names, and every entry in the taxonomy carries an explicit target alongside its name. This is the same point as scoring (issue, actor) pairs rather than articles — it just bites at the taxonomy layer first, before a single article has been scored.

Review the "none of these" bucket weekly. When something recurs in it, you have found a genuine new issue, and adding it is a deliberate, versioned decision rather than a silent drift.

One consequence is easy to miss and expensive. The forty-odd standing subjects are written by hand and a person can check every one. Stories are not. Thirty are born a day, each needing a line saying what a favourable score would be favourable towards, and most of those lines will be written by a model. Same trap as the net zero rollback, sprung daily, and invisible on the finished picture. Two defences: a story borrows the wording from its subject wherever it plainly belongs to one, so only new things need new wording; and a person reads every new one once a day, which should take a minute. The direction check sits underneath, but it works at subject level, so it catches a run of bad wording rather than a single one.

Sentiment

The word "sentiment" is hiding three different things

This is where projects like this usually go wrong, so it's worth being pedantic. When someone says "how is the Mail reporting immigration", they could mean any of three things, and only one of them is interesting.

Tone

Is the language negative? Almost always yes — it's news. A classifier run over a piece about a rail crash returns "negative" from every outlet, which tells you the crash was bad and nothing about the newspaper. Cheap to compute, nearly worthless on its own.

Stance

Given an issue and an actor, is this article favourable, unfavourable, or neutral towards them? This is the one that carries information, and it is the only thing worth spending model tokens on. Score the pair, never the article alone.

Salience

How much are they running on it, and how prominently? Front page, or page 14? Salience is often more revealing than stance — an outlet's strongest editorial signal is frequently what it declines to cover at all. It is also the only one of the three that the obvious source cannot give you — worth being clear about now rather than discovering in week six.

A feed gives you counts, not prominence. An RSS item carries a headline, a link, a summary and a timestamp. There is no field for how big the story was: a splash across the top of the page and a one-line link buried two thirds of the way down arrive looking identical. Counting articles, normalised to each outlet's own daily output, is what you get for nothing — and it is the weakest of the three signals.

A homepage gives you prominence directly, because the layout is the editor's ranking made visible. News homepages are built from a handful of discrete typographic ranks — splash, lead, secondary, list item — so rendering the page and clustering the headline type sizes recovers that ranking without having to understand any particular site's markup, and survives redesigns in a way CSS selectors do not. Absolute sizes must never be compared between outlets, because the Guardian's splash type is not the Sun's. What travels across outlets is the tier index within a page.

Two things follow, and both are load-bearing. The first is that prominence and persistence are different measurements with different availability: how big a story is right now can be read from a single snapshot, but how long an outlet keeps it up can only be accumulated by looking repeatedly, and a poll you did not make is gone. The second is that a homepage fetch is exactly the request publishers defend, so for a good part of the list the direct route is closed — which is what the archive section above is for.

Ship all three, labelled separately, and let the reader see that the grid's colour is stance and its size is salience. Collapsing them into one "sentiment score" would be the easy call and the wrong one.

How to score it

Use an LLM with a written rubric rather than a lexicon — the task needs to understand that "a crackdown on X" is favourable to one actor and hostile to another, and lexicons can't do that. Return structured output per (article, issue): a score from −2 to +2, a confidence, and a verbatim supporting quote. The quote is not decoration; it's what makes a wrong score debuggable and what you put on the drill-down page.

Score blind. Strip the outlet name, byline, and any masthead-identifying boilerplate before the text reaches the model. This costs you nothing and removes the most obvious line of attack — that the model already "knows" what the Telegraph thinks and scores accordingly.

Calibration, which is not optional

A tracker that assigns political stance to named British newspapers will be accused of bias in its first week. The defence has to be built in from the start, and it is not a disclaimer — it's a measurement.

  • Hand-label a gold set of 300–500 articles, with at least two annotators working independently.
  • Report inter-annotator agreement. If humans can't agree, the model can't be right, and you need a better rubric before you need a better model.
  • Publish model-versus-human accuracy per issue, and show confidence intervals in the UI rather than a bare decimal.
  • Re-run the gold set on every prompt or model change and keep the history. Drift is real and silent.
  • Add a direction test. Pick a handful of issues whose left-right ordering is not in doubt — the net zero rollback, the BBC licence fee, farm inheritance tax, small boats — and assert the sign of the left-minus-right difference on every run. A mis-specified stance target does not blur a score, it inverts it, and on a red-to-green globe an inverted issue is simply the wrong colour. This check catches it in seconds rather than after a month of archive that would have to be re-scored.
  • Put the rubric, the gold set, and the changelog on a public methodology page. Nobody will read it; its existence is what does the work.
Stack & cost

Sixteen dollars a month

No server, no database, no paid hosting. Python in a GitHub Actions runner (feedparser for the feeds), embeddings from a small sentence-transformers model on the runner's own CPU, gzipped JSONL shards in the repo, and a static front end on GitHub Pages. Actions minutes are unlimited on a public repo and the hourly 06:00–22:00 schedule fits the free allowance on a private one either way.

The model bill is the only line that scales, and one decision dominates it. Headline plus standfirst is about 70 tokens an article, while the rubric and issue taxonomy are around 1,200 — so sending one article per call means paying seventeen times more for prompt than for content. Batch about 25 articles against one shared rubric and the same work costs a fifth as much.

Volume scales it linearly: ~$8 at a thousand items a day, ~$31 at four thousand. The Batch API would halve it again, but at these numbers the 24-hour turnaround is not worth $8 — keep it for backfills.

Never re-score. Key every result by content hash and check before calling. Actions runners are ephemeral and rebuild state each run, so a pipeline that regenerates rather than resumes will re-bill the entire archive every hour. This is the one mistake that turns $16 into a runaway bill.

One cost that is not monthly: changing the rubric means re-scoring the archive to keep the time series comparable. A year at 2,000 a day is ~730,000 articles, about $190 at list or $95 batched. Budget for one or two a year.

Build order

Nine weeks, in the order that de-risks it

The order matters more than the schedule. Each phase produces something you can look at and be wrong about — which is the only way to find out that your issue taxonomy is bad before you have built a front end on top of it.

Three things are structural, meaning they are cheap now and expensive in three months: the stance target on every issue, because getting it wrong inverts a score rather than blurring it; the frame contract, because playback needs geometry that is written as you go and cannot be recomputed afterwards; and the source list, because an outlet you did not collect in March is gone. Everything else — the palette, the layout, the copy, the views — can be redone in an afternoon and should not be allowed to hold up the first three.

Four decisions before any code

  • The taxonomy, with targets. About thirty issues, each carrying a target field naming what stance is favourability toward, plus a written polarity expectation. "Net zero rollback" is not a topic, it is a direction.
  • The frame contract. What one hour looks like on disk, including the fitted seed positions and weights. Decided now because the archive is written from the first day of collection and a frame without geometry is a frame you cannot animate.
  • The source list and how you lawfully get the text. Not a detail: the reality check in this document found Metro, the Guardian, the BBC, the Telegraph and the Express all blocking the crawler. Headline and standfirst from each outlet's own feed is the fallback, and it is thinner than the rubric assumed.
  • The scoring unit. An (issue, actor) pair, scored on headline plus standfirst, not an article and not a sentence.
01
Week 1 · plumbing

Prove the premise holds

Feeds for every outlet on the list, SQLite, a scheduled job, a daily CSV. No models, no UI, no clustering. Two questions only: how many genuinely distinct articles per outlet per day actually arrive, and how much of that is the same agency copy wearing different mastheads? Audit every robots.txt in the same week and write down, per outlet, exactly what you are allowed to fetch — feed and homepage, which are different permissions. Settle the Guardian archive question here too, and pin the archived URL form for every outlet, because between them those two decide what salience can claim.

Ships A spreadsheet that tells you whether there is a product here at all.
02
Week 2 · the thing that inverts

Taxonomy, rubric, gold set

Write the thirty issues with their stance targets. Write the rubric. Then label three hundred items by hand, twice, before any model sees them — the labelling exercise is how you discover the rubric is ambiguous, and it is far cheaper to find that out now than after a month of archive. Add the direction regression test in the same week: a handful of issues whose left-right ordering is not in doubt, asserting the sign of the difference. It catches an inversion the day it appears.

Ships A defensible rubric and an inter-annotator agreement number.
03
Week 3 · structure

Clustering and issue assignment

Local sentence embeddings, agglomerative clustering into stories, stories mapped onto the fixed taxonomy. The part that is easy to underestimate: story identity across days. A cluster that gets a new id every morning cannot be tracked, and every downstream view depends on it. Ids are assigned by matching against yesterday's centroids before new ones are minted.

Ships Yesterday's news as thirty-odd named, persistent issues.
04
Week 4 · the model

Scoring and calibration

Haiku over the Batch API, one call per (issue, actor) pair, returning stance, salience and tone with a confidence. Then calibrate against the gold set and publish the number, including the failure modes. An uncalibrated score on a red-to-green sphere is a very confident-looking guess.

Ships A scored day and an honest accuracy figure.
05
Week 5 · the archive

The frame writer

The phase that is invisible and decides whether any of the time views are possible. Every hourly run writes a frame: per-issue share, stance mean and spread, breadth, live-set membership, each outlet's homepage tier for each issue — and the fitted seed positions and weights, warm-started from the previous frame so a cell stays where it was. Prominence belongs in this phase for the same reason the geometry does: dwell is the difference between two consecutive snapshots, and nobody can compute it later. Stamp each frame with the taxonomy version that produced it. Write monthly gzipped shards. Do this from the first day of collection, because a frame you did not write is a frame you cannot go back for.

Ships An archive that can be scrubbed, growing by itself.
06
Weeks 6–7 · the point of it all

The front end, on real data

Port the prototype above: the sphere, the three levels beneath it, one paper's own globe, the comparison chart and playback. Most of the hard problems are already solved and written down here — warm-started refitting, a slowly-varying radius key, the area floor, damped labels, stable ink. What is left is real data, both themes, mobile, reduced motion, and the equivalent table for anyone the graphic does not serve.

Ships A public page that updates itself hourly.
07
Week 8 · trust

Traceability and methodology

Every score on the page traceable in two clicks to a headline, a link and the exact text it was scored on. A methodology page carrying the rubric, the accuracy figures, the known failure modes and the colour-vision note. This is the week that converts a provocative graphic into something people are willing to cite, and it cannot be retrofitted after the first argument about bias.

Ships The version you can defend in public.
08
Week 9 · operations

Make it survive not being watched

Alert on silent source loss — an outlet that quietly stops appearing looks exactly like an outlet that has gone quiet, and the sphere will keep drawing a confident picture either way. Alarm on token spend. Write the backfill runbook now, while you still remember why the taxonomy is versioned: a taxonomy change is a bounded re-score, not an edit, and it costs $95–190 for a year of archive.

Ships Something that keeps working while you are on holiday.

Shape of the repository

  • ingest/ — one module per outlet, a shared fetch policy, robots compliance, dedup.
  • cluster/ — embeddings, story formation, story identity across days.
  • score/ — the rubric as a prompt, batch submission, the calibration harness.
  • frames/ — the fitter and the frame writer. The only place layout maths lives.
  • web/ — the static front end. Reads shards, renders, never calls an API.
  • data/ — gzipped monthly shards, committed. The archive is the asset; keep it in the repository where it cannot be lost.
  • tests/ — the gold set, the direction regressions, and the layout invariants: no empty cells, no overlapping labels, area error within tolerance.

Two scheduled workflows. The hourly one, 06:00 to 22:00, fetches, clusters, scores the deltas, writes a frame and commits. The nightly one re-runs the day whole, recalibrates, runs the regressions and rebuilds the month's shard — hourly work is allowed to be approximate as long as something more careful comes along behind it.

What I would cut first

If nine weeks becomes five: cut the paper globe, cut the comparison chart, cut every view below the story level. Do not cut the frame writer, the stance targets, the gold set or the traceability. The first four are features and can be added whenever; the last four are either right from the first commit or they are a rebuild.

Risks

What will bite you

Wire copy inflates everything

A single PA story runs near-verbatim in six outlets. Counted naively, that reads as consensus and six times the volume, when it's one piece of journalism.

Fix Deduplicate on a content fingerprint before counting, keep the canonical copy, and flag the rest as syndicated. Show syndication rate as its own number — it's interesting in its own right.

Volume is not attention

MailOnline publishes many times what the FT does. Raw article counts will make it look like the Mail cares about everything and the FT about nothing.

Fix Normalise to share of each outlet's own daily output, not absolute counts. Weight by homepage tier wherever you can read one — directly where the outlet permits it, from the archive where it does not — and where you can read neither, mark that outlet as counts-only on the page itself. Three grades of salience, honestly labelled, beats one grade dishonestly implied.

Headline scoring runs hot

Headlines are written to be sharp, so headline-only scores skew more negative than full-text ones. Since every outlet is now scored the same way this cannot misrepresent one title against another, but it can tilt the whole scale.

Fix Calibrate the offset against full text on the outlets that give it to you, and subtract it. Re-measure whenever the rubric changes.

Everyone will assume you're biased

Publishing stance scores for named British newspapers is a political act whether you intend it or not, and both directions of complaint will arrive.

Fix Blind scoring, a published rubric, an open gold set, visible confidence intervals, and a changelog. Argue about method, never about conclusions.

Red-to-green fails for one man in twelve

Red-to-green is the natural scale for sentiment and it is what this design uses. It is also the axis red-green colourblindness flattens. Simulating deuteranopia and protanopia across 38 candidate palettes, every one with poles of equal lightness scored 3–11 on pole separation — one flat colour to a colourblind reader.

Fix Keep red-to-green, but make the two ends differ in lightness. The separation score tracks the lightness gap almost exactly, and the contrast-heavy variants score 16–61 on the same measure. Carry the stance number on the label as well, so colour is never the only cue.

Publishers block crawlers

Tested live on 8 Sept 2026: Metro, the Guardian and the BBC all refused Anthropic's crawler; Sky's RSS answered. Blocking is per publisher and per user agent, and it is getting more common, not less.

Fix Check robots.txt per outlet for the agent you will actually use, before promising the outlet list. Treat a blocked outlet as excluded and say so on the page — a missing outlet must never read as an outlet with no coverage.

Silent source loss

A feed changes URL, an adapter starts returning nothing, and the grid quietly shows an outlet covering nothing at all. That reads as an editorial finding rather than a bug.

Fix Per-source health checks and a hard rule: an outlet with no items in six hours renders as "no data", visually distinct from "no coverage".
